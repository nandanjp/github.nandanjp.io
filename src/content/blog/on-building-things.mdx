There's a specific kind of paralysis that hits when you're building something for yourself. You're the user, the product manager, the engineer, and the critic — all at once. Every decision has infinite time to second-guess.

I've been building things for long enough to know the pattern: the more latitude you have, the slower you ship.

## The perfection trap

When you're building for a deadline or a user, constraints are your friend. The deadline cuts the decision space. The user's feedback grounds you. You ship, you learn, you iterate.

When you're building for yourself — especially something like a personal site — there's no external forcing function. You can always refactor the component structure *one more time*. You can always find a better animation. The site is never quite right.

The only way out is to accept that imperfect and shipped beats perfect and perpetually in-progress.

## What I mean by "building"

I don't just mean writing code. I mean the whole arc: deciding what to build, actually building it, shipping it, and then living with the decision.

The last part is underrated. You learn more from the things you shipped and had to maintain than from anything you polished endlessly in private.

## A practical rule

Ship the thing that's 80% of what you envisioned. Then see if the remaining 20% actually matters.

It usually doesn't. Or it turns into something different than you imagined — something better, because you learned from using it.

---

This site exists because I stopped waiting to build the *perfect* version and just built *a* version. I'll improve it over time. Or I won't. Either way, it exists now, and that's worth something.
